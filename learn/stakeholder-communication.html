🗣️ Explaining AI to non-technical people

EnglishHinglish

🏠 Real-life example

A good CA doesn't explain tax law section by section to a shop owner. They say: 'If you do X, you save ₹40,000 this year, and here's the one risk.' Explaining AI to business people works the same way: lead with the outcome, use their words, and be honest about risks.

💡 In simple words

Forward deployed engineers, PMs and consultants spend much of their time with people who don't care about embeddings. They care about time, money, risk and their customers. Structure updates as: the outcome or decision needed, the evidence (a demo or numbers), the risks and how they're handled, then next steps. Replace jargon with analogies. Show a working prototype early, since it beats ten slides. Listen more than you talk in discovery meetings, and confirm requirements in writing. Be upfront about what AI can't do: trust is your most valuable asset.

🪜 Step by step

  1. Start with the outcome they care about: time saved, money, risk, customers.
  2. Explain how it works with one everyday analogy, not technical terms.
  3. Be honest about limits: how often it's wrong, and what protects against that.
  4. Show, don't tell: a short demo on their real data convinces more than slides.
  5. End with a clear decision or next step you need from them.

🔑 Key terms

Stakeholder
Anyone affected by, or deciding on, the project.
Discovery
Meetings to understand the real problem, users and constraints.
Executive summary
The 3–4 line version: outcome, evidence, risk, ask.

👀 See it in action

text
❌ "We implemented hybrid retrieval with a re-ranker and improved MRR@10 by 0.18."
✅ "The assistant now finds the right policy 9 times out of 10, up from 7.
    Agents save ~4 minutes per ticket (≈ ₹6 lakh/month).
    Risk: rare wrong answers → it always shows its source.

Same work, explained as outcome → evidence → risk → ask.

⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

  • Overpromising ("it will be 100% accurate"). It destroys trust at the first mistake.
  • Drowning people in metrics they can't interpret. Translate numbers into impact.

✅ Quick check

What should you lead with when updating a business leader?

  1. Your tech stack
  2. Model architecture details
  3. The business outcome and the decision you need
Show answer

C. The business outcome and the decision you need: Leaders decide on outcomes and risks. Give them that first, and details on request.

🙋 Questions learners ask

How do I explain accuracy to a business leader?

In counts and consequences: "Out of 100 invoices, it reads 95 correctly; the other 5 are flagged for a person to check."

What if a stakeholder is afraid AI will replace their team?

Acknowledge it, show how the tool removes boring work, involve the team in testing, and share how roles will change.

🎯 Asked in interviews on this topic

Try answering out loud first, then open the model answer.

A customer says, "We want to use AI." What do you do in the first meeting?

💬

I don't start with AI; I start with their problem. I ask: what is the most painful or expensive process right now? Who does it, how often, and how is success measured? What data and systems are involved? Then I'd pick one or two use cases that are high value, have data available and are low risk if the AI is imperfect, and agree on a success metric, like "reduce invoice processing time by 50%". I end the meeting with a clear next step: a small proof of concept with real data, within a few weeks.

✅

  • Problem first: pain, frequency, owner, current metric
  • Data and systems available
  • Choose use cases by value, feasibility and risk
  • Agree a measurable goal and a small next step

: Customer discovery. They watch whether you ask before you solve.

A customer insists on a feature you believe won't work. How do you handle it?

💬

First, understand why they want it; often the request hides a real need that can be met another way. Then I share my concern with evidence, not opinion: for example, "in our test on 100 of your documents, fully automatic approval was wrong 12% of the time". I offer an alternative that meets the underlying goal, like automatic drafts with one click approval. If they still want to try, I propose a small, time boxed pilot with agreed success criteria, so the data decides. I keep it respectful and focused on their outcome.

✅

  • Find the underlying need
  • Use data, ideally their data
  • Offer an alternative that meets the goal
  • Time boxed experiment with agreed criteria

: Influence without authority; customer maturity.

Your AI demo fails in front of the customer. What do you do?

💬

Stay calm and be honest: "That's a wrong answer, so let me show you why it happened." Then use it: open the trace, show which document was retrieved and where it went wrong, and explain how we'd catch this with our evaluation set and fix it. Customers trust people who understand their system's failures more than people who pretend there are none. Afterwards, I'd add the case to the eval set, fix it, and follow up with the result. And next time, I'd rehearse with their real data and have a recorded backup.

✅

  • Stay calm, acknowledge it
  • Turn it into a demonstration of control: trace, explain, fix plan
  • Follow up with the fix
  • Prevention: rehearse with real data, backup recording

: Composure and honesty under pressure.

A logistics company wants to reduce missed deliveries. How would you approach it?

💬

Clarify before solving: how is a missed delivery defined, how many happen, what do they cost, and what data exists, like GPS, delivery attempts, addresses and customer contacts? Break the problem into causes: bad addresses, customer not home, routing and time windows, and capacity. For each, size the impact using their data, then propose the quickest win. For example, an LLM plus geocoding to clean and verify addresses at order time, and a model predicting no shows to trigger a confirmation message the day before. Define a metric, run a pilot in one city with a control group, then scale. Throughout, I'd check what operations staff would actually use.

✅

  • Clarify definitions, scale, cost and data
  • Decompose into root causes; size each with data
  • Pick quick, high impact interventions (AI and non AI)
  • Pilot with a control group; a metric; scale up

: The decomposition case is the key filter round for FDE roles.

Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate or stakeholder.

💬

Show you disagree with data and respect. "A product manager wanted to launch our AI reply feature to all customers. I was worried because our tests showed it sometimes made up refund policies. Instead of just saying no, I showed five real examples and the error rate from our eval set, and proposed launching to 10% of tickets with agent approval first. We agreed on criteria to expand. Two weeks later the error rate was below our threshold and we expanded. We kept a good relationship because we focused on the shared goal."

✅

  • Situation and both perspectives fairly
  • You used evidence, not authority
  • A compromise or experiment
  • Outcome and the relationship afterwards

: Collaboration and influence.

Explain a technical concept to me as if I'm not technical.

💬

Pick something from your work and use an everyday analogy. For example, explaining RAG: "Imagine a very smart new employee who has read a lot but knows nothing about our company. If you ask them a policy question, they might confidently guess. RAG is like giving them the right pages of our policy manual before they answer, and asking them to point to the page. So answers are based on our actual documents, and you can check them." Then check understanding: "Does that make sense?"

✅

  • Everyday analogy
  • No jargon, or jargon explained
  • Why it matters to the listener
  • Check understanding

: Communication, essential for FDE, PM and senior roles.

📌 Remember

Outcome → evidence → risk → ask. Analogies over jargon. Demos over slides.

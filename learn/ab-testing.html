🆎 Metrics and A/B testing for AI features

EnglishHinglish

🏠 Real-life example

A chai stall owner wonders if a new ginger recipe sells better. Switching completely and comparing with last month is unfair, because weather and festivals change sales. Instead, for two weeks they randomly serve the old recipe to half the customers and the new one to the other half, then compare. That's an A/B test.

💡 In simple words

Before launching an AI feature, define its success metric (resolution rate, time saved, conversion), guardrail metrics that must not get worse (complaints, cost, latency), and how big an improvement matters. An A/B test randomly splits users into control (old) and treatment (new) groups so that the only systematic difference is the feature, which lets you claim causation. Run it long enough to reach statistical significance, and don't peek and stop early. For AI, combine offline evals before launch with online A/B results after it.

🎬 See how it works

Users are split at random: half see the old version (A), half the new one (B). Because the split is random, the difference in results is caused by the feature.

🪜 Step by step

  1. Write a hypothesis: "AI reply drafts will cut handling time by 15% without lowering satisfaction."
  2. Pick one primary metric and a few guardrail metrics that must not get worse.
  3. Randomly split users (or agents) into control and treatment, and estimate how many you need and for how long.
  4. Run the full planned duration without peeking and stopping early.
  5. Analyse the difference with confidence intervals, check guardrails and segments, then decide.

🔑 Key terms

Control / treatment
The group without the change vs the group with it.
Guardrail metric
A metric that must not get worse while you optimise another.
Statistical significance
Confidence that a difference isn't just random luck.

👀 See it in action

text
Feature: AI-suggested replies for support agents
Success:   handling time  9.1 → 6.8 min (treatment) ✅ significant
Guardrail: CSAT           4.4 → 4.4                 ✅ unchanged
Guardrail: cost/ticket    +₹1.2                     ✅ within budget
Decision: roll out to 100%

One success metric, guardrails, and a clear go / no-go call.

⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

  • Stopping as soon as results look good. Early peeks produce false wins.
  • Testing many metrics and celebrating whichever moved. Decide the primary metric beforehand.

✅ Quick check

Why randomly assign users in an A/B test?

  1. So the feature is the only systematic difference between groups
  2. To make the test shorter
  3. It's easier to code
Show answer

A. So the feature is the only systematic difference between groups: Randomisation balances everything else, so the difference can be attributed to the feature.

🙋 Questions learners ask

What is statistical significance in simple words?

How unlikely the difference would be if the change had no real effect. It doesn't tell you the effect is big or valuable.

What if we can't randomise?

Use a phased rollout by region or team, compare before and after carefully, and be honest that the evidence is weaker.

🎯 Asked in interviews on this topic

Try answering out loud first, then open the model answer.

What's the difference between correlation and causation? How would you prove causation?

💬

Correlation means two things move together; causation means one actually causes the other. Ice cream sales and drownings are correlated because both rise in summer, not because ice cream causes drowning. In products, users who use a new feature might retain better simply because they were already more engaged. To show causation, the best tool is a randomised A/B test: randomly give the feature to half the users, so the only systematic difference is the feature, then compare outcomes.

✅

  • Correlation ≠ causation; hidden third factors (confounders)
  • Selection bias: who opts in is different
  • Randomised experiment is the gold standard

: Statistical thinking for decisions; critical for PMs and analysts.

How do you structure an end-to-end ML system design interview, such as feed ranking or search?

💬

I use a six step structure. First, clarify the business objective, latency budget (say 150 ms) and scale. Second, frame the ML task and choose offline metrics (like Recall@100 and NDCG@10) plus online north-star and guardrail metrics. Third, describe the data and feature store: user history, item metadata, and real-time session signals joined point-in-time without leakage. Fourth, use a two-stage funnel: fast candidate generation (two-tower embeddings + BM25 narrowing millions of items to 500) followed by a rich GBDT or neural ranker scoring those 500, plus a re-ranker for diversity and business rules. Fifth, cover cold start and position bias. Sixth, cover shadow/A-B rollout and drift monitoring.

✅

  • Clarify business goal, scale and latency budget first
  • Offline ranking metrics (Recall@K, NDCG) vs online business & guardrail metrics
  • Two-stage architecture: fast candidate retrieval → feature-rich ranker → business rules
  • Address cold start, position bias, point-in-time features and monitoring

: Tests whether you can design a complete production ML funnel rather than just talking about a loss function.

A new model looked better offline but you're worried about production. How do you roll it out safely?

💬

Step by step. First, shadow mode: the new model receives real traffic and makes predictions, but users still get the old model's output, so I can compare predictions, latency and errors safely. Then a canary: send a small share, like 5%, of traffic to the new model and watch technical and business metrics. If it holds up, increase gradually, or run a proper A/B test. Keep the old model ready, so rollback is one switch. Define the success and rollback criteria before starting.

✅

  • Shadow deployment
  • Canary with small traffic, then ramp up or A/B test
  • Predefined success and rollback criteria
  • One click rollback

: Risk management in production.

How would you define success metrics for an AI feature?

💬

In layers, so no single number hides problems. Model quality: accuracy, hallucination rate or rubric scores on an eval set. User outcome: did it help? Task completion, time saved, how much users edit the AI's output. Adoption and engagement: how many eligible users try it and come back. Business: revenue, cost per ticket or retention. Plus guardrail metrics that must not get worse, like complaints, latency and cost per use. A very accurate feature nobody uses is still a failure, and a popular one with a high error rate is a risk.

✅

  • Layers: model quality, user outcome, adoption, business
  • Guardrails: errors, latency, cost, complaints
  • Leading vs lagging metrics
  • No single metric stands alone

: The most common AI PM analytical question.

Design a recommendation system for an e commerce site with 2 million products.

💬

Clarify: where recommendations appear, the goal (clicks, purchases), latency budget and data available. Use two stages. Candidate generation: quickly narrow 2 million products to a few hundred, using collaborative filtering or two tower embeddings plus simple sources like popular in category. Ranking: a more detailed model, like gradient boosting or a neural ranker, scores candidates using user, product and context features, then business rules handle stock and diversity. Cold start: new users get popular and contextual items; new products use content features like category and text embeddings. Evaluate offline with recall and NDCG, then online with an A/B test on purchases. Serve with precomputed embeddings and a feature store for fresh features.

✅

  • Clarify goal and latency
  • Two stage: candidate generation, then ranking
  • Cold start for users and items
  • Offline metrics, then A/B test; serving with feature store

: Classic ML system design.

We launched an AI tutor. Under 10% of users love it, most find it useless. What do you do?

💬

First, understand the split. Who are the 10%: which grades, subjects or use cases? And what does "useless" mean for the rest: wrong answers, too generic, too slow, or just not what they needed? I'd read real conversations, look at where users drop off, and talk to both groups. If the 10% share a clear use case, like exam revision in maths, I'd double down there: improve quality for that case with evals, and change onboarding so new users start with it. For the rest, fix the top failure reasons or stop showing the feature to segments where it doesn't fit.

✅

  • Segment the lovers vs the rest
  • Diagnose with conversations, funnels, interviews
  • Double down on the use case that works
  • Fix top failures or narrow the audience

: Analytical product thinking on a messy AI launch.

Your AI feature has launched. How do you know if it's actually working, and how do you improve it?

💬

I measure in layers. The outcome the feature exists for, for example handle time or tickets resolved without escalation, proven with an A/B test or a holdout group. Usage: adoption, retention and frequency. Behaviour based quality: accept rate, how much users edit drafts, regenerate rate, abandonment and escalation, plus thumbs feedback with a reason, which on its own is a weak signal. Guardrails: complaints, safety flags, latency and cost must not get worse. Every week we sample real interactions under our privacy rules, label failures by type, and turn the top types into new eval cases and fixes. That loop keeps the feature improving after launch.

✅

  • Outcome metric proven with A/B or holdout
  • Usage: adoption, retention, frequency
  • Behaviour signals like edit and accept rate beat thumbs alone
  • Guardrails: complaints, safety, latency, cost
  • Weekly failure review feeding the eval set

: Product sense and ownership after launch.

📌 Remember

Define success and guardrails first, then randomise and wait for significance.
